# C4. Attribution & Originality

## The Originality Question

When AI helps create something, who created it?

This isn't a philosophical puzzle — it has practical implications:
- Can you claim this as your work?
- Do you need to disclose AI use?
- Is it original enough to copyright?
- What do you owe to audiences, clients, or collaborators?

## The Contribution Spectrum

AI-assisted work exists on a spectrum:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                   CONTRIBUTION SPECTRUM                      │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                             │
│  100% Human ←──────────────────────────────→ 100% AI        │
│                                                             │
│  • Wrote every word        • Edited AI draft      • Prompt  │
│  • AI suggested, rejected  • 50/50 blend          • Accept  │
│  • Used AI for research    • Heavy AI + light edit          │
│                                                             │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Most AI-assisted work falls somewhere in the middle — and that's fine. The question is: where exactly, and does it matter for your context?

## When Disclosure Matters

### Professional Contexts

| Context | Expectation | Recommended Disclosure |
|---------|-------------|----------------------|
| **Journalism** | High scrutiny | Disclose AI assistance |
| **Academic writing** | Varies by venue | Check policies, disclose |
| **Marketing copy** | Generally accepted | Usually not required |
| **Fiction** | Unclear norms | Emerging — consider context |
| **Client work** | Depends on contract | Discuss with client |
| **Personal blog** | Your choice | Optional but honest |

### Contractual Obligations

Some agreements explicitly address AI:
- "Work must be original"
- "No AI-generated content"
- "Disclose use of automation"

Read your contracts. When in doubt, ask.

### Audience Expectations

Consider what your audience assumes:
- A "handcrafted" newsletter implies human writing
- A personal essay implies personal experience
- An expert opinion implies expert thinking

Violating expectations — even if technically allowed — can damage trust.


## Copyright and Ownership

### The Current Legal Landscape (2025)

**Key developments:**
- US Copyright Office: Pure AI output (no human authorship) is not copyrightable
- Human selection, arrangement, and modification can establish copyright
- The more human creative input, the stronger the copyright claim
- Laws vary by jurisdiction and are evolving rapidly

**Practical implication:** The more you do beyond "generate and accept," the clearer your claim to the work.

### Strengthening Your Claim

Actions that demonstrate human authorship:
- Substantial editing and revision
- Creative selection from multiple options
- Original framing, structure, or arrangement
- Integration of original content
- Creative direction and iteration

Actions that weaken your claim:
- Accepting output with minimal changes
- Simple prompt → accept workflow
- Using AI to replicate existing works

### When It Matters

Copyright matters most when:
- You want to license or sell the work
- You need to defend against copying
- You're creating for commercial use
- Others might claim the same work

For personal or low-stakes use, this may be less relevant.

## The Ethics of Creative AI

Beyond legality, there are ethical considerations:

### Transparency

**Principle:** Don't deceive about how something was made.

This doesn't mean constant disclosure — but don't actively mislead. If someone asks, answer honestly.

### Fair Competition

**Principle:** Don't claim advantages you don't have.

Using AI to produce more isn't unfair. Claiming you're a faster writer when AI is doing the writing is misleading.

### Creative Integrity

**Principle:** Be honest with yourself about what's yours.

The work you're proudest of should feel earned. If heavy AI use leaves you feeling like a fraud, that's worth examining.

### Impact on Others

**Principle:** Consider how AI use affects other creators.

Using AI to undercut human creatives on price — legal. Ethical? Worth thinking about.

## Practical Attribution Approaches

### For Published Work

Options for disclosure:

**Minimal:**
> "AI tools assisted in the preparation of this article."

**Standard:**
> "This piece was drafted with AI assistance (Claude) and edited by the author."

**Detailed:**
> "AI was used to generate initial drafts and suggest structural improvements. All content was verified and substantially revised by the author, who takes responsibility for the final work."

### For Client Work

Discuss upfront:
- Whether AI use is acceptable
- What disclosure (if any) is required
- Who owns the work
- How AI use affects pricing (if relevant)

Document agreements in writing.

### For Collaborative Projects

When working with others:
- Agree on AI policies at project start
- Be transparent about your workflow
- Ensure everyone's comfortable with the approach

## Originality in the AI Age

### What Makes Work Original?

Even with AI assistance, originality can come from:

**Concept:** The idea, angle, or insight
**Selection:** Choosing what to include, exclude, combine
**Voice:** How ideas are expressed
**Arrangement:** Structure, flow, framing
**Context:** Applying ideas to specific situations
**Judgment:** Evaluating what works

AI can help execute. The creative decisions remain human.

### The "Would This Exist Without Me?" Test

Ask yourself:
- Did I bring something unique to this?
- Does this reflect my perspective, taste, or judgment?
- Would someone else with the same prompt get the same thing?

If AI could produce this with a generic prompt from anyone, it's not very original. If it required your specific vision, knowledge, or taste, that's different.

### Embracing the Hybrid

Rather than "AI vs. human," think "AI + human":

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                   THE HYBRID CREATOR                         │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                             │
│  What AI contributes:        What you contribute:           │
│  • Speed and volume          • Vision and intent            │
│  • Options and variations    • Selection and judgment       │
│  • Execution assistance      • Meaning and purpose          │
│  • Pattern completion        • Pattern breaking             │
│                                                             │
│  Together: More than either alone                           │
│                                                             │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The goal isn't to hide AI use — it's to create work that wouldn't exist without both the AI's capabilities and your creative direction.

## Key Takeaways

- Originality exists on a spectrum — most AI-assisted work is collaborative
- Disclosure expectations vary by context — know your field's norms
- Copyright favours human creative input — the more you do, the stronger your claim
- Transparency builds trust — don't actively deceive about AI use
- Originality comes from concept, selection, voice, judgment — not just execution
- The hybrid model: AI contributes speed and options; you contribute vision and meaning
- Be honest with yourself about what's genuinely yours

## Case Study: The Writers Guild and the 2023 Strike Settlement

From May to September 2023, the Writers Guild of America (WGA) strike resulted in the first major AI provisions in creative industry labour agreements. The outcome matters for how you think about attribution and originality.

**Key Provisions:**

- Companies cannot require writers to use AI
- AI-generated material cannot be classified as "literary material" or used to reduce writer credits
- Writers can use AI as a tool with company permission, but the writer remains the author
- AI-generated content does not count towards minimums for employment classifications

In parallel, the US Copyright Office confirmed: "Prompts alone do not provide sufficient human control" for copyright ownership. Human authorship is required.

**Why This Matters:**

The guild negotiation didn't reject AI. It protected the principle that **the human author retains agency and credit**. This is precisely what the contribution spectrum in this module identifies: where does human creativity and control sit on the continuum?

The legal system answered: if there's meaningful human authorship and control, you have copyright. If it's prompt-and-accept, you don't.

**Key Takeaways:**

1. The attribution principles in this module are already being codified in law and contracts
2. The "human as author" principle survived its first major legal test
3. Professional bodies move faster than legislation — guild agreements set norms before courts catch up

You're learning principles that are becoming enforceable obligations.

- https://www.copyright.gov/ai/
