# Ed1. AI-Resistant Assessment Design

## The Problem with Authentic Tasks

For years, assessment design meant: "Create tasks that measure the skill we want learners to develop."

Now it means something harder: "Create tasks that measure the skill, knowing that AI can do many things learners were supposed to learn to do."

This isn't a crisis. It's a redesign challenge.

The core question: What matters more — that learners can *produce* the output, or that learners can *think, judge, and decide* about that output?

## The Spectrum: From "AI Can Do This Entirely" to "AI Can't Help at All"

Not all skills sit on the same spectrum with respect to AI:

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                 THE AI CAPABILITY SPECTRUM                   │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                              │
│  AI FULLY CAPABLE              MIXED CAPABILITY          BEYOND AI
│  (Writing essays)         (Designing experiments)    (Reading humans)
│  (Solving equations)      (Editing for voice)        (Real judgment)
│  (Summarising)            (Code architecture)        (Genuine novelty)
│  (Basic coding)           (Strategic decisions)      (True creativity)
│                                                              │
│  ← Easier to automate     Middle ground     Hardest to automate →
│                                                              │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Your assessment design challenge: Where does your skill sit on this spectrum, and what does that mean for assessment?

**Left side (AI can do it):** You need to assess thinking about the output, not just the output itself.

**Right side (AI struggles):** You can assess output more directly, but add AI-literacy challenges.

**Middle (Mixed):** Layer assessments — assess the parts AI can't do easily.

## Process-Based vs. Product-Based Assessment

The traditional distinction now has new importance.

### Product-Based Assessment (Traditional)

Assess the final output:
- The essay, report, or solution
- The code, design, or proposal
- The presentation or analysis

**The AI problem:** AI can produce credible outputs in many domains. An A-grade essay written by AI looks like an A-grade essay. A well-structured report written by AI is well-structured.

**The redesign:** Add visibility into *process*. Don't just grade the essay — assess:
- How they chose their sources
- What they reconsidered and why
- Where they disagreed with initial findings
- How they verified claims

### Process-Based Assessment (Increasingly Essential)

Assess the thinking, not just the result:
- How did they approach the problem?
- What trade-offs did they consider?
- How did they decide between options?
- Where did they second-guess themselves productively?

**Examples:**
- Annotated drafts showing revision decisions
- Reflection documents explaining choices
- Decision logs showing what was considered and rejected
- Step-by-step working shown, not just final answer
- Peer feedback they gave and how they responded

This is harder to fake with AI alone. You can ask AI to write an essay, but you can't easily ask AI to annotate your thinking process convincingly over weeks.

```
QUIZ:
You want to assess whether learners can write persuasive emails. A learner submits an excellent email written mostly with AI help. What's the most useful assessment approach?

* Grade the email quality — if it's good, they've learned the skill
* Require learners to show drafting process: their original outline, AI's suggestions, and their edits
*! Ask learners to explain their persuasion strategy, show what they tried first, and reflect on what worked
* Assume AI help and give partial credit

FEEDBACK: When output is easily generated by AI, shift to assessing the thinking behind it. Process-based assessment makes the learner's understanding visible — and harder to fake.
```

## Authentic Assessment in an AI World

Authentic assessment means tasks that resemble real work — not contrived school tasks. This is now your strength.

### Realness Is Your Advantage

In the real world:
- People have access to tools
- Thinking matters more than keystroke-by-keystroke production
- Judgment about what output is *good* matters more than producing output
- Iteration and refinement are normal
- The task isn't "produce a document" but "solve a problem that might involve a document"

**You can now assess real work legitimately.**

Instead of: "Write a 500-word essay on climate policy without tools."

Try: "Research a local environmental decision being debated in your area. Explain the trade-offs different groups see, including scientific, economic, and social factors. Show your sources. Explain why you found some arguments more persuasive than others."

This task:
- Allows AI use (realistic)
- Can't be done well by prompt-and-accept (requires judgment)
- Is harder to fake because it's specific to local context
- Assesses real skills: research, analysis, perspective-taking

### Shifting the Locus of Assessment

Move assessment from "Can you produce output?" to:
- "Can you recognise good output?"
- "Can you improve output?"
- "Can you judge when output is good enough?"
- "Can you recognise when to use a tool vs. when not to?"
- "Can you explain your reasoning?"

### Practical Examples

**From:** Write a marketing email
**To:** Here are three different marketing emails (one written by a human, one by basic AI, one by a professional copywriter trained with AI). Rank them. Explain which works best for [specific audience]. Revise the weakest one. Explain your improvements.

**From:** Solve these 20 algebra problems
**To:** Here are different solution methods to this problem type. Explain which method is most efficient for this type of problem. When might you choose a different method? Create a similar problem that would trick someone using the most common method.

**From:** Write a lab report
**To:** Here's data from an experiment. Ask for AI suggestions on interpretation. Evaluate the AI suggestions — which are sound? Which miss something? Write the report incorporating the parts that work and explaining why you rejected others.

```
EXERCISE:
Take one assessment task you currently use. Map it onto the spectrum above. Where does it fall? Then redesign it to shift assessment from product to process:

1. What's the final output in this task?
2. What thinking does AI make invisible?
3. How can you make learners' thinking visible? (Annotations, reflections, decisions, comparisons, justifications)
4. What would an authentic version of this task look like in the real world?

Draft one redesigned assessment. What stays? What changes?
```

## Assessment in Practice

### The Transparency Principle

Whatever you ask learners to do, be transparent about:
- Whether AI use is expected, permitted, or forbidden (and why)
- What aspects of the task require their own thinking
- What aspects can be delegated
- How you'll know if they've learned vs. if they've used AI

### Avoiding the Gotcha

Don't design assessment around *catching* AI use. That's a game learners will lose eventually anyway (AI will improve). Instead, design tasks where demonstrating learning *requires* thinking that AI can't do for them.

### The Levels Approach

Design assessments with multiple levels:

**Level 1 (Foundational):** Can learners use the tool at all? "Use AI to draft a proposal outline."

**Level 2 (Intermediate):** Can they evaluate and improve output? "Use AI drafts as a starting point. Rewrite to match [specific context/audience/requirements]."

**Level 3 (Advanced):** Can they make strategic decisions? "When would you use AI for this task? When wouldn't you? Show the thinking process."

This acknowledges that learners are at different points in learning to work with AI.

```
QUIZ:
A learner submits work that's clearly AI-generated with minimal human involvement. Rather than marking it wrong, what's more educationally useful?

* Give it a low mark and explain that's not acceptable
*! Have a conversation about why they chose to rely on AI here, and what they'd need to do to demonstrate their own learning
* Assume they cheated and escalate
* Accept it and count it as evidence they can delegate well

FEEDBACK: The goal is learning, not punishment. A learner who relies entirely on AI is revealing something — a gap in their own understanding, confidence, or understanding of the task. That's diagnostic feedback. Use it to guide next steps.
```

## Key Takeaways

- The assessment spectrum: Tasks vary in how much AI can do them. Design accordingly.
- Shift from product to process: Grade the thinking, not just the output. Use annotations, reflections, and reasoning shown.
- Authenticity is your advantage: Real work allows tools. Assess the judgment about how to use them.
- Transparency: Be clear about expectations. Don't trap learners; teach them to think.
- Layered assessment: Assess what matters most at the skill level where it matters most.
- The locus of learning: Has it shifted from "produce output" to "judge and improve output"? Your assessment should reflect that.
