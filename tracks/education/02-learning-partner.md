# Ed2. AI as Learning Partner

## The Outsourcing Trap

AI is genuinely helpful. It's fast, available, and competent. This creates a real danger: learners can use it in ways that *feel* productive but actually prevent learning.

The trap: "I asked AI to do it, and it worked. Am I learning?"

Sometimes yes. Often no.

## When AI Helps Learning vs. When It Replaces It

Understanding the difference is core to using AI pedagogically.

### AI Helps Learning When It:

**Provides scaffolding** — breaks complex tasks into pieces
- Example: "Break down the steps for writing a lab report. I'll do the steps myself, with your guidance."

**Offers contrast** — shows different approaches so learners compare
- Example: "Show me five different ways to structure an argument. Why would each work for different contexts?"

**Provides feedback** — responds to the learner's attempt
- Example: "I drafted an argument. What's weak about it? What would make it stronger?"

**Removes routine barriers** — so focus stays on the skill
- Example: "I need to write persuasive text. I'm not focused on typing speed, so help me refine ideas fast."

**Extends practice** — generates varied examples
- Example: "Generate 10 similar math problems at this difficulty. I'll solve them."

### AI Replaces Learning When It:

**Answers instead of guiding** — learner gets the result without thinking
- Example: "Write an essay about climate policy." [Learner accepts and submits]

**Removes productive struggle** — learner never sits with difficulty
- Example: "I don't know how to solve this. Can you show me the answer?"

**Creates false fluency** — learner thinks they understand because AI made it seem easy
- Example: "The code works, so I must understand algorithms."

**Encourages copying** — learner uses AI output as their own without processing
- Example: "AI wrote this report. I just fixed typos. That's done."

**Skips the difficulty** — where learning actually happens
- Example: "This is confusing. AI can do it for me." [Never returns to understanding]

The difference often comes down to: *Does the learner think, or does the AI think for them?*

```
QUIZ:
A learner is stuck on a maths problem. They could ask AI to solve it, or ask AI for hints. Which approach supports learning better?

* Ask AI to solve it — they can learn from the solution
*! Ask AI for hints — they do the thinking with guidance
* Either works equally well
* Ask the teacher — AI gets in the way

FEEDBACK: Hints and guidance support learning. Answers short-circuit it. The struggle to solve the problem is where mathematical thinking develops.
```

## Socratic Use Patterns: AI as Questioner

The Socratic method: answering questions with questions, helping learners discover answers themselves.

AI can do this. Not perfectly, but usefully.

### Pattern 1: The Question Chain

Instead of asking AI to explain something, ask AI to ask you:

> "I'm learning about evolution. Don't explain it. Instead, ask me probing questions that would help me understand the key concepts. After each answer I give, ask a follow-up."

This works because:
- You have to think to answer
- Confusion becomes obvious immediately
- The learning is yours, the guidance is AI's

### Pattern 2: The Socratic Challenge

Ask AI to play learner, while you teach:

> "Pretend you're a learner who doesn't understand fractions. I'll explain something. Ask me questions like a learner would. If I'm unclear, let me know."

This works because:
- You have to articulate ideas clearly
- You hear what's confusing from an outside perspective
- Teaching is learning

### Pattern 3: The Guided Discovery

Ask AI to ask progressively harder questions:

> "Generate 5 questions about this case study, progressing from basic understanding to analytical thinking. I'll answer each one. Then we can see where my understanding breaks down."

This works because:
- You get immediate feedback on comprehension
- Difficulty increases as understanding grows
- The struggle is yours to own

### Pattern 4: The Devil's Advocate

Ask AI to challenge your thinking:

> "Here's my argument for [position]. Find the weakest parts. Ask me questions that would make someone doubt this argument."

This works because:
- You have to defend your ideas
- Weak reasoning becomes visible
- You're strengthened by the challenge

```
EXERCISE:
Pick a concept you're teaching. Design a Socratic interaction:

1. Write three questions a learner might ask about it
2. For each, draft a question-in-response (not an answer) that would guide them to understanding
3. Try this pattern with learners: "I'll ask you questions. Answer honestly. If you can't answer, we've found what to work on together."

How does this change the learning interaction?
```

## Scaffolded Independence: Less AI as Skills Develop

Scaffolding is support that decreases as competence increases. This applies to AI use too.

### The Scaffolding Arc

**Novice:** Heavy AI scaffolding
> "Generate an outline for a persuasive essay. I'll write each section myself, using your structure."

**Developing:** Less scaffolding
> "I've drafted an essay. Where's my argument weakest? Help me strengthen it, but I'll write the revision."

**Competent:** Minimal scaffolding
> "I'm writing an essay on this topic. Tell me if my logic works."

**Advanced:** Strategic use only
> "Are there assumptions I'm missing in this argument?"

The goal: learners gradually internalise the processes AI was guiding. They become better at questioning themselves, checking their work, finding weaknesses — the skills AI was modelling.

### Designing the Fade

Make the scaffolding structure explicit:

| Stage | Task | AI Role | Learner Does |
|-------|------|---------|--------------|
| **1** | Learn the process | Guides heavily | Observes, then tries |
| **2** | Build confidence | Provides structure | Fills in content |
| **3** | Develop skill | Offers feedback | Revises independently |
| **4** | Demonstrate mastery | Steps back | Works alone or checks own work |

Learners should know they're moving through stages. Transparency helps them see progress.

### The Danger of Permanent Scaffolding

Scaffolding that never fades becomes dependency.

If learners always ask AI to structure their thinking, they never learn to structure it themselves. If they always ask AI to find weaknesses, they never develop that critical eye.

Build in explicit *withdrawal* points:

> "For the first three problems, I'll help you structure your solution. For problems 4–6, you structure it; I'll check your work. For 7–10, you're on your own — and then we'll look at your work together."

This tells learners: the support decreases intentionally. It's not because you're abandoning them; it's because they're getting stronger.

```
QUIZ:
A learner has been using AI to outline their essays for weeks. They now understand essay structure well. What should happen next?

* Keep using AI for outlines — consistent approach
*! Gradually shift to outlining themselves, with AI reviewing their structure
* Completely remove AI — learn to write without any tools
* It depends on the learner's comfort level

FEEDBACK: Competence should lead to independence. Fade scaffolds deliberately. Use AI less as learners demonstrate they can do more themselves.
```

## The Role of Productive Struggle

This is the hardest part of AI-assisted learning to get right.

### What Productive Struggle Is

**Productive struggle:** The learner encounters a problem they can't immediately solve. They try multiple approaches. Some don't work. Eventually they either solve it or learn something from attempting it. The struggle itself builds capability.

**Unproductive struggle:** The learner is confused and has no clear path forward. They're stuck, discouraged, and not learning. Just frustrated.

The difference is subtle but crucial.

### Why Struggle Matters

Neural science suggests learning involves:
- Trying something that doesn't work
- Recognising the failure
- Revising your approach
- Succeeding through effort, not just instruction

When AI removes struggle entirely, it removes something essential to learning.

### The AI Interference Risk

AI makes everything easy. That's its strength and education's challenge.

- A struggling student can ask AI, and the struggle disappears.
- But so does the learning that the struggle would produce.

### Calibrating Difficulty

Your role shifts from "remove all difficulty" to "calibrate difficulty":

**Too easy:** No struggle, no learning
**Calibrated:** Productive struggle with support available
**Too hard:** Unproductive struggle, hopelessness

With AI in the picture, you need to be more intentional:

> "Work on this problem for 10 minutes without AI help. If you're stuck after that, we'll talk through your thinking together. If you're still stuck after that, AI can help."

This structure:
- Protects productive struggle (10 minutes alone)
- Prevents unproductive struggle (support available)
- Develops persistence
- Uses AI strategically, not reflexively

### Signs of Productive Struggle

- Learner is trying different approaches
- Learner is asking good questions about why something didn't work
- Learner is making progress, even if slowly
- Learner shows frustration but continues trying
- Learner is learning something even if they don't solve the immediate problem

### Signs of Unproductive Struggle

- Learner has stopped trying
- Learner is cycling through the same failed approach
- Learner is discouraged and disengaged
- Learner has no theory about why something isn't working
- Learner is spending energy on emotion rather than problem-solving

When you see unproductive struggle, that's when to intervene — with a hint, a question, or (if needed) AI scaffolding.

```
EXERCISE:
Observe a learner working on something challenging. After 5 minutes, notice:
- Are they trying different things, or stuck on one approach?
- Are they asking useful questions about what's not working?
- Do they seem engaged or just frustrated?
- What would be useful support right now?

This is calibration. Over time, you get better at knowing when to let struggle continue and when to intervene.
```

## When to Use AI in Learning

This is the practical question teachers ask:

**Use AI when:**
- Learner needs scaffolding they can't get from a teacher
- The task needs multiple examples, and generating them manually is slow
- The learner's struggle is with routine (formatting, structure, finding sources) not with core concepts
- The learner is developing metacognitive skills (awareness of their own thinking)
- The learner is practising feedback integration (receiving input and revising)

**Don't use AI when:**
- The skill being learned *is* the process AI would do (e.g., learning to write by having AI write)
- The learner hasn't yet tried the task themselves
- The struggle is the entire point (e.g., learning to persist)
- You want to see what the learner actually thinks, not what AI thinks
- The learner is using AI to avoid rather than to learn

## Key Takeaways

- The outsourcing trap: AI can do the task, but does it help the learner learn?
- Socratic patterns: Use AI to ask questions, not answer them
- Scaffolded independence: Less AI support as learners develop competence
- Productive struggle matters: Some difficulty is essential to learning
- Calibrate, don't eliminate: The right amount of support, not the easiest path
- Strategic use: AI as tool for thinking, not substitute for thinking
