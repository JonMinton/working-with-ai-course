# Ed3. Personalisation & Differentiation

## The Promise and the Limits

AI personalisation often sounds like magic: tailor every learner's experience to their exact needs. Generate unlimited practice problems at the right difficulty. Create materials in every language. Adapt explanations to every learning style.

The reality: AI can help with personalisation. It can't be personalisation. And the limits matter.

## Using AI to Adapt Materials

This is where AI is genuinely useful.

### Pattern 1: Difficulty Variants

Create one core task. Ask AI to generate variants:

> "Here's a problem at Grade 5 level. Generate versions at:
> - Grade 3 (simpler)
> - Grade 5 (current)
> - Grade 7 (more complex)
> - AP level (advanced)"

Then you review them. Some will be good. Some won't. But you've created optionality in minutes rather than hours.

This works for:
- Maths problems
- Reading passages
- Case studies
- Coding challenges
- Writing prompts

### Pattern 2: Accessibility Variants

Start with standard material. Ask AI to create accessible versions:

> "Here's a science explanation. Create versions:
> - Simplified vocabulary, same concepts
> - With key terms highlighted
> - With diagrams described in text
> - Broken into shorter paragraphs
> - As a Socratic dialogue instead of exposition"

Again, review the outputs. Some will work perfectly. Some will need adjustment. But you've created multiple ways into the same content.

### Pattern 3: Language Variants

If you're teaching multilingual learners:

> "This English text explains photosynthesis. Create accurate translations in [Spanish, Arabic, Mandarin]. Prioritise clarity over literal translation."

Caveats: AI translation varies in quality. A native speaker should review. But quick access to materials in home languages is valuable.

### Pattern 4: Style Variants

Different learners engage with different framing:

> "Here's a lesson on fractions. Create versions framed as:
> - A real-world problem (sharing pizza)
> - A mathematical exploration
> - A game with rules
> - A history of why fractions matter"

Not all learners learn the same way from the same framing. Options help.

## Creating Variants: Difficulty Levels

Difficulty progression matters. Not all "simpler" versions are actually simpler — sometimes they're just shorter, or they remove important nuance.

### A Better Difficulty Spectrum

**Level 1 (Foundation):** Simpler context, smaller numbers, fewer steps
- Example (Maths): "5 + 3 = ?"
- Example (Writing): "Write one sentence describing this picture."

**Level 2 (Developing):** More realistic context, slightly larger numbers, some complexity
- Example: "You have 5 apples. Your friend gives you 3 more. How many do you have now?"
- Example: "Write a paragraph describing this picture. Use at least one adjective."

**Level 3 (Proficient):** Real-world context, typical complexity, multiple steps
- Example: "A fruit stand has 5 apples and 3 oranges. If 2 fruits are sold, how many remain? Show your work."
- Example: "Write a descriptive paragraph about this picture. Explain what's happening and why it matters."

**Level 4 (Advanced):** Complex context, multiple possibilities, open-ended thinking
- Example: "A fruit stand has 5 apples and 3 oranges, worth 50p and 60p respectively. Propose pricing that maximises profit while staying competitive."
- Example: "Write a reflection on this picture: What story does it tell? What questions does it raise?"

When you ask AI to generate difficulty variants, reference this spectrum explicitly. And review: does "Level 1" actually feel like a foundation version? Or is it just truncated?

## Customisation: Where Teacher Judgment Still Matters

Here's where AI has hard limits: **knowing your students.**

### What AI Doesn't Know

**Their specific learning history**
- AI doesn't know that Maya struggles with fractions but excels at word problems
- AI doesn't know that Jamal is avoiding maths because he had a bad experience last year
- AI doesn't know that Sophie learns best through movement and collaboration

**Their interests and identities**
- A generic problem about shopping is boring to most learners
- A problem about gaming might engage some, offend others
- Context matters more than content

**Their social dynamics**
- AI doesn't know that this learner is more confident in pairs, not groups
- AI doesn't know that two learners work well together or terribly together
- AI doesn't know who needs explicit encouragement and who needs independence

**Their specific gaps**
- AI can identify that "this learner got 3 of 5 problems wrong"
- AI can't identify *why* — is it conceptual confusion? Careless arithmetic? Rushing? Anxiety about maths?

### What Only You Know

You know:
- What questions to ask to reveal confusion
- Which learners need challenge vs. support right now
- When a learner is ready to move on vs. when they need to consolidate
- How to motivate specific learners
- Which contexts will resonate with which students
- The social dynamics of your classroom

**This is not something AI personalisation can replace.** It's what human teaching is.

### The Productive Partnership

AI handles:
- Generating multiple options (materials, problems, explanations)
- Scaling what would otherwise be manual work (creating variants)
- Removing routine barriers (formatting, organising, processing)

You handle:
- Knowing which option is right for which learner
- Spotting the real cause of confusion, not just the symptom
- Deciding when a learner is ready, when they need help, when they need challenge
- Building relationships of trust that make personalisation meaningful
- Adapting in real time based on what you see in the moment

```
EXERCISE:
Think about a learner you know well — someone you've taught for weeks or months. List what you know about them that AI couldn't know without months of interaction:

- Their learning history (what's worked for them before?)
- Their confidence patterns (when do they engage? When do they withdraw?)
- Their interests (what contexts engage them?)
- Their relationships (who do they work well with?)
- Their gaps (where specifically do they struggle?)

This is the foundation of real personalisation. AI tools can support it, but can't replace it.
```

## Privacy and Ethical Considerations

Personalisation often involves collecting data about learners. This deserves serious thought.

### What Data Are You Collecting?

When you use AI tools with learner data, consider:

**Identifying information:** Names, ages, locations, school IDs
- Higher risk; more regulation applies

**Learning data:** Test scores, problem attempts, time-to-solve
- Medium risk; useful for personalisation, but should be secure

**Behavioural data:** Engagement patterns, time spent, browsing history
- Medium-high risk; can reveal sensitive information

**Demographic data:** Gender, socioeconomic status, language background
- High risk if used to make assumptions; valuable if used to prevent bias

### Key Questions

Before using a tool for personalisation:

1. **What data does it collect?** Identify information, learning data, behavioural data?
2. **Who owns this data?** The school? The vendor? Shared?
3. **How is it stored and encrypted?** Is it secure?
4. **Who has access?** Just the teacher? Administrators? The vendor?
5. **How long is it retained?** Forever? Until graduation? Deleted?
6. **Can learners and parents see it?** Can they request deletion?
7. **Is it used for anything else?** Sold? Used to train AI? Shared with other tools?
8. **What's the legal basis?** GDPR, FERPA, or local regulations?

**Your responsibility:** Understand what you're agreeing to. If the vendor can't or won't answer these questions, that's a red flag.

### Protecting Student Privacy

Practical steps:

- **Minimise data:** Use only what you actually need
- **Anonymise when possible:** Let AI work on "student 1" not "Marcus Johnson"
- **Be transparent:** Tell learners and families what data is being collected and why
- **Secure sensitive data:** If using an online tool, ensure it meets your school's security standards
- **Audit the vendor:** What do they do with the data? Can you verify it?
- **Have an exit plan:** If you stop using a tool, what happens to learner data?

## Reducing Bias Through Personalisation

AI can help reduce some educational biases — if used carefully.

### The Bias Risk

Educational AI can amplify existing biases:
- If training data reflected historical inequities, the AI will too
- If a tool assumes all learners have similar backgrounds, it will disadvantage those who don't
- If learners are categorised early, they may be tracked into lower expectations

### How Personalisation Can Help (or Harm)

**Helpful:**
- Offering multiple modalities (visual, auditory, kinesthetic) helps all learners, especially those whose learning style didn't match traditional instruction
- Removing time pressure can reveal knowledge that timed tests obscure
- Personalising difficulty levels helps high-achieving learners avoid boredom and struggling learners avoid hopelessness

**Harmful:**
- Assuming learners from certain backgrounds need "simpler" explanations
- Using demographic data to predict ability
- Personalising learners into different tracks before they've had equal opportunity to learn
- Assuming that optimisation for "average" learner helps everyone

### Mitigation

When designing personalisation:

- **Be intentional about diversity:** Create variants that reflect different contexts, perspectives, and identities
- **Audit for assumptions:** Does the tool assume things about learners based on group membership?
- **Keep humans in charge of tracking:** AI might recommend tracking, but you decide if that's appropriate
- **Review outcomes:** Are some learner groups progressing faster than others? Is that because of bias or because of actual need?
- **Make pathways visible:** Learners should know why they're on a particular difficulty level, and how to move to another

## Key Takeaways

- AI helps generate variants: difficulty levels, accessibility, language, style — but all need review
- AI can't personalise like teachers do: it doesn't know learners' histories, interests, gaps, or social dynamics
- The partnership: AI creates options; you choose which is right for each learner
- Privacy matters: Know what data is collected and who has access to it
- Bias can hide: Check that personalisation systems don't assume things about learners based on group membership
- Teacher judgment is essential: Personalisation without knowing students isn't personalisation — it's just option-generation
