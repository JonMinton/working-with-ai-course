# Ed4. Teaching AI Literacy

## Why Now?

A generation of learners will work in a world shaped by AI. Many will use it daily. Some will build it. All need to understand it.

Teaching AI literacy isn't about making learners into AI researchers. It's about developing judgment: when to use AI, when not to, how to evaluate AI output, how to think critically about claims AI makes.

These are essential skills. And they're learnable.

## Building AI Collaboration Skills: The Core Competencies

What should learners actually know?

### 1. How to Use AI (Practically)

This is the baseline. Learners should be able to:
- Formulate a clear prompt
- Refine a prompt based on results
- Evaluate whether output is good
- Integrate AI output into their own work
- Know when to ask AI vs. when to ask a teacher
- Recognise what AI is better and worse at

**Teaching approach:** Hands-on. Give learners real tasks and have them work with AI. The learning is in the doing.

**Example activities:**
- Write prompts to generate practice problems
- Refine prompts until output is what they wanted
- Evaluate whether AI's output is accurate
- Use AI drafts as a starting point and revise

### 2. How AI Works (Conceptually)

Learners don't need to understand neural networks. They do need to understand:
- AI makes predictions based on patterns in data
- It can't see things not in its training data
- It's fast at pattern matching, not at true understanding
- It can sound confident while being wrong
- Different AI systems have different capabilities and limitations

(Core Module 1: How AI Actually Works covers these ideas — including hallucination and sycophancy — in more depth, and is a useful refresher before teaching them.)

**Teaching approach:** Analogies and experiments. Compare AI to other systems.

**Example analogies:**
- "AI is like an incredibly well-read student who can predict what comes next in a sentence really well, but hasn't truly understood what it means."
- "AI is like a very sophisticated autocomplete — it's predicting what's most likely to come next, not thinking."
- "Training data is like a textbook the AI learned from. It's brilliant at finding patterns in that textbook, but can't see outside it."

**Example experiments:**
- Feed AI the same prompt twice and see if results differ
- Ask AI to solve a problem and explain its reasoning
- Ask AI a question it can't answer well and notice how it tries anyway
- Change one word in a prompt and see what changes in output

### 3. Evaluating AI Claims (Critically)

This is crucial. Learners should be able to:
- Spot when AI is making things up
- Recognise confident-sounding but false statements
- Distinguish between "AI can do this sometimes" and "AI can reliably do this"
- Understand what makes a good AI application vs. a marketing claim
- Ask "Would this actually work in practice?"

**Teaching approach:** Scepticism and evidence. Show examples of AI failing, over-claiming, or producing plausible nonsense.

**Example activities:**
- Ask AI to explain something it's actually bad at and have learners spot the problems
- Compare AI's explanation to human expert explanations
- Fact-check AI claims using reliable sources
- Evaluate an AI application and discuss whether it would actually work

### 4. Impact Awareness

Learners should understand:
- AI systems can reflect and amplify bias
- AI affects labour, creativity, privacy, power
- There are genuine trade-offs (faster vs. more thoughtful, convenience vs. privacy)
- Different people are affected differently by AI
- They have choices about how to use AI

**Teaching approach:** Discussion and perspective-taking.

**Example activities:**
- Discuss: "If an AI system can predict credit risk well, is it ethical to use? What if it systematically disadvantages some groups?"
- Research: "How is AI being used in [industry]? Who benefits? Who's affected?"
- Perspective-taking: "How would a teacher feel about students using AI? A librarian? A writer? A student with a learning disability?"

## Age-Appropriate AI Education Frameworks

AI literacy looks different at different ages.

### Early Primary (Ages 5–7)

**Focus:** Basic observation and pattern-finding

- What are computers good at? (Fast, remembering lots, following instructions)
- What are humans good at? (Creative thinking, caring, understanding context)
- Simple exposure: "This tool helped me organise the classroom. How did it do that?"

**Activities:**
- Sorting tasks (AI excels; humans find exceptions)
- Pattern games (AI-like and human-like thinking)
- Watching what AI does without fully understanding how

**Not appropriate yet:**
- Neural networks, machine learning concepts
- Complex ethical trade-offs
- Independent AI use

### Upper Primary (Ages 8–11)

**Focus:** How AI helps and how humans stay in charge

- AI follows patterns in data
- Humans tell AI what task to do
- Some things AI does well; other things humans do better
- AI needs human oversight to avoid mistakes

**Activities:**
- Using AI with clear guidance (teacher operates; learners observe and evaluate)
- Feeding simple data to AI and discussing patterns
- Experimenting with "What if I change this?" (prompts, inputs)
- Discussing: "Is AI a good choice for this task?"

**Not appropriate yet:**
- Independent decision-making about AI use
- Ethical dilemmas (too complex)
- Training their own AI systems

### Lower Secondary (Ages 11–14)

**Focus:** Using AI effectively and evaluating its outputs

- How to formulate a clear request (prompts)
- AI can be wrong confidently
- Different AIs have different strengths
- When to use AI and when not to
- Privacy and consent matter when AI uses data

**Activities:**
- Hands-on use with real AI tools
- Comparing outputs from different AI systems
- Fact-checking AI claims
- Discussing privacy in age-appropriate terms
- Using AI for authentic learning tasks (not just "see what it can do")

**Not appropriate yet:**
- Deep technical understanding
- Complex policy discussions
- Specialised AI applications

### Upper Secondary (Ages 14–18)

**Focus:** Critical evaluation and informed decision-making

- How machine learning works (conceptually)
- Limitations of AI and where bias can hide
- Trade-offs in AI applications (accuracy vs. fairness, privacy vs. personalisation)
- Impact on different groups
- Ethical frameworks for AI decisions
- Where learners' own values matter

**Activities:**
- Evaluating real AI applications
- Researching AI in a field of interest
- Debating ethical questions
- Designing responsible AI applications
- Understanding policy and regulation

**Can introduce:**
- Technical careers with AI
- Policy and ethics discussions
- Training simple models (with guidance)

*! Developing good judgment about when AI is helpful and when it's not

## Building Critical Evaluation Skills

The most important competency is scepticism tempered with nuance. Learners should ask good questions.

### Teaching Critical Questions

Teach learners to ask:

**About the AI itself:**
- What is this AI trained on?
- What's it designed to do well?
- What might it be bad at?
- How often is it wrong?
- Have humans tested it for bias?

**About the output:**
- Does this seem right?
- How would I verify this?
- What could be wrong with this answer?
- Where might bias hide?
- What's the AI not considering?

**About the context:**
- Is AI the right tool for this job?
- What would a human do differently?
- Who benefits from using AI here?
- Who's disadvantaged?
- What could go wrong?

**About claims AI makes:**
- How does the AI know this?
- Is this something AI is actually good at?
- Is this overconfident language?
- Can I check this against a reliable source?

### Practice Activities

**Activity 1: The Claim Evaluation**

Present AI output that sounds plausible but might be wrong:

> "The AI says that the capital of Australia is Sydney. Is this right? How would you verify it? Why might the AI have said this?"

Learners research and discover it's wrong. Discussion: Why would AI make this mistake? (It's in lots of text as an important Australian city, so a pattern-matching system predicts it.)

**Activity 2: The Confidence Calibration**

Ask an AI to do something it's bad at:

> AI: "The colour of the number 7 is green. I am 95% confident."

Discuss: The AI sounds confident. But is it right? How do we know when to trust AI's confidence level? (We don't. This is why we verify.)

**Activity 3: The Bias Hunt**

Ask AI to generate something and look for bias:

> "Generate a paragraph about a successful businessperson."

Compare multiple responses. Whose perspective is missing? What assumptions appear? (Gender, age, background, type of business assumed?) This teaches learners to spot patterns they should question.

**Activity 4: The Ethical Scenario**

Present a realistic dilemma:

> "A school uses AI to predict which Year 7 students will graduate. Those with low predictions get placed in a lower academic track. The AI is 80% accurate overall. Should the school use it? Why or why not? What could go wrong?"

Discussion surfaces: accuracy isn't the only criterion; fairness matters; early categorisation can create self-fulfilling prophecies; different people are affected differently.

## The Meta-Skill: Learning to Learn with New Tools

The deepest competency is this: **learners should be able to develop competence with new tools, learning from experience.**

This matters because AI will change. The specific tools learners learn to use might be obsolete in five years. But learning to learn with new tools will serve them forever.

### Developing Learning Agility with Tools

Teach learners to:

**Experiment:** Try things. See what works.
- "Mess around with this tool for 10 minutes. What can it do? What can't it do?"

**Reflect:** Think about what works and why.
- "What was your first prompt? Did it work? What did you change?"

**Iterate:** Refine based on results.
- "The AI output wasn't quite right. What would you ask differently?"

**Transfer:** Apply learning to new tools.
- "You know how to use AI for writing. What would transfer to using AI for coding?"

**Adapt:** Adjust your approach based on what you learn.
- "This tool is bad at X but great at Y. What would you use it for?"

### Teaching Through Diverse Tools

If you teach learners to use only one AI tool, they learn that tool. If you expose them to several different tools (even briefly), they learn the principle.

**Activity:** Over a term, introduce learners to:
- A text-based AI
- An image AI
- A code AI
- A voice AI
- A search tool enhanced with AI

For each, ask the same questions:
- What's it designed to do?
- What's it bad at?
- How do you ask it things?
- How do you evaluate output?

**The insight:** The specific tools vary. The principles of use transfer.

## Responsibility and Impact

Teaching AI literacy isn't morally neutral. You're helping learners develop judgment about tools that affect society.

Include conversations about:

**Consequences of AI choices:**
- "If we use AI for hiring, what happens to people who don't fit the patterns?"
- "If we use AI to recommend content, what filter bubbles might form?"
- "If AI is trained on historical data, what past injustices does it learn?"

**Their own agency:**
- Learners have choices about how to use AI
- They can question AI applications they disagree with
- They can advocate for responsible AI use
- Not all AI applications should exist (just because we can doesn't mean we should)

**Power and access:**
- Some people have access to advanced AI; others don't
- Some jobs will be affected more than others
- AI decisions affect people who never chose to use it
- It's worth caring about this

This isn't preachy. It's realistic. AI will affect their lives, jobs, creativity, and society. They should have frameworks for thinking about that.

```
QUIZ:
A company releases an AI hiring tool that's 90% accurate at predicting job performance. It's faster and cheaper than human review. Should companies use it?

* Yes — it's accurate and efficient
* No — you should never use AI for hiring
*! Maybe — accuracy isn't the only question. Is it fair? Does it disadvantage some groups? Are there better alternatives? What would you need to know?
* It depends on the company

FEEDBACK: Good critical thinking about AI isn't black and white. It considers multiple factors: accuracy, fairness, impact, alternatives, values. Learners should practice this nuanced thinking.
```

## Key Takeaways

- AI literacy includes: practical use, conceptual understanding, critical evaluation, and impact awareness
- Age-appropriate teaching: Early years focus on observation; older learners develop judgment and critical thinking
- Critical evaluation is the core skill: learners should ask good questions about what AI claims
- Learning agility matters more than tool-specific knowledge: teach learners to develop competence with new tools
- Include ethical and impact considerations: learners should understand that AI choices have consequences
- Make it real: teach AI literacy through authentic tasks, not abstract concepts

## Case Study: Ethan Mollick's Seven-Role Framework

Ethan Mollick, Associate Professor at Wharton and a leading researcher on AI in education, and his colleagues have proposed a set of distinct AI roles in learning, each with different pedagogical benefits and risks.

**The Seven Roles:**

1. **AI-tutor** — Explains concepts, answers questions
2. **AI-coach** — Provides feedback and guidance
3. **AI-mentor** — Offers career or skill advice
4. **AI-teammate** — Collaborates on projects
5. **AI-tool** — Completes specific tasks (like writing)
6. **AI-simulator** — Simulates realistic scenarios for practice
7. **AI-student** — Acts as a learner; students teach it

**Why This Matters:**

The framework helps teachers choose the right AI role for the right learning moment. They're not all the same. Using AI as a tutor is different from using it as a simulator or as a student.

In his book "Co-Intelligence," Mollick argues for collaborative intelligence rather than replacement. This mirrors the framework in this module: AI is a tool for learning, not a substitute for the teacher.

**One Particularly Powerful Idea:**

Having students use AI as a "student" (explaining concepts back to it, catching when it's confused) is one of the most promising strategies Mollick and colleagues describe. This turns AI from a potential replacement into a learning amplifier.

**Key Takeaway:** Not all AI use in education is the same. The role matters enormously. Teachers need a vocabulary for AI pedagogy, and this framework provides it.

Learn more: https://www.oneusefulthing.org/

## Case Study: An Institutional AI Literacy Programme

Consider how a large university — Manchester Metropolitan is one widely discussed example — can approach AI literacy at scale: a substantial, dedicated budget for a programme designed to reach the large majority of teaching staff (figures in accounts of such programmes vary; treat any you encounter as illustrative).

**The Approach:**

Rather than optional workshops, this kind of programme implements:
- Foundation workshops for all staff
- Faculty-specific communities of practice (engineering, business, humanities had different needs)
- Assessment redesign grants to help educators rethink how students demonstrate learning

**Why This Matters:**

Institutional change doesn't happen through encouragement alone. It requires investment, structure, and incentives. Institutions that invest in this way do so because they understand that helping teachers develop AI pedagogy isn't a nice-to-have — it's essential.

**Key Takeaway:** Teaching AI literacy at scale requires institutional commitment: funded time, structured support, and incentives for innovation. A few voluntary workshops won't shift practice systemwide.
